## Lactulose: Acute urate nephropathy

## One-Sentence Summary

Lactulose is a non-absorbable sugar originally used to treat hepatic encephalopathy, hepatic coma and constipation.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be useful in **obstructive jaundice** (mainly to reduce gut endotoxin-related complications around surgery), with **0 registered clinical trials** and **20 publications** retrieved for this direction. Most of these are animal, experimental or small clinical studies, and the evidence is early-stage.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known information, lactulose is a non-absorbable disaccharide. It is fermented in the colon by gut bacteria, which acidifies the gut contents and changes the intestinal flora. This underlies its established use in hepatic encephalopathy and constipation.

In obstructive jaundice, bile salts are absent from the gut. This promotes bacterial overgrowth, endotoxin absorption and intestinal barrier dysfunction, which are linked to postoperative kidney dysfunction and liver injury. Lactulose may reduce the gut endotoxin load and bacterial translocation. A 1986 clinical study of 24 patients found lower endotoxaemia in the lactulose group (portal and systemic samples, P<0.05). A 1991 multicentre randomised study of 102 patients compared preoperative lactulose, bile salts and no treatment for preventing postoperative renal dysfunction. Several rat studies examined bacterial translocation and renal or hepatic outcomes.

The evidence is mixed. One review notes that a kidney-protective effect of lactulose has not been conclusively shown in clinical studies. A rat study found that lactulose did not prevent bile infarction or transaminase elevation. Any benefit would therefore be supportive (fewer perioperative complications), not disease-modifying.

Other predictions are weaker. Acute urate nephropathy, nephrolithiasis and exercise-induced malignant hyperthermia have no trials or literature, so their high scores are not supported. Bile duct disease largely inherits its evidence from obstructive jaundice. Biliary tract disease, hyperphosphatemia and bile duct neoplasm have only indirect evidence.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold (research question only)**

**Rationale:**
There is a plausible gut-endotoxin mechanism and some small clinical and animal studies in obstructive jaundice. However, no clinical trials are registered, the clinical results are mixed, and the only New Zealand licence has been cancelled. The remaining predictions have little or no supporting evidence.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Full-text review of the 1986 and 1991 clinical studies to confirm design and results
- Mechanism-of-action data and the Medsafe package insert (warnings and contraindications)
- A current New Zealand market or supply pathway, since the only licence is cancelled
- A focused trial or systematic review of preoperative lactulose in obstructive jaundice, with renal outcomes and endotoxaemia as endpoints
- Review of the knowledge-graph contraindications (especially diabetes and galactosemia) for any proposed patient population
