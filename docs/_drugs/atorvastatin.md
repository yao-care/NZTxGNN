## One-Sentence Summary

Atorvastatin is a statin, a cholesterol-lowering medicine that blocks HMG-CoA reductase.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be useful for **HIV infectious disease**, mainly through effects on immune activation, inflammation and cardiovascular risk rather than direct antiviral action.
The data compiled for this report include **13 clinical trials** and **20 publications**. The trials are small, and most are early-phase or terminated.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism-of-action data are not available in the data compiled for this report. Atorvastatin is a member of the statin class, which lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking cholesterol synthesis in the liver.

In people with HIV, the rationale rests on three points:
- **Lipid lowering:** Protease-inhibitor-based antiretroviral therapy often raises cholesterol and triglycerides, and statins are a standard way to treat this.
- **Anti-inflammatory effects:** Statins may reduce monocyte/macrophage activation and immune activation. This could lower cardiovascular risk and possibly help HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders.
- **Interaction with antiretrovirals:** Protease inhibitors and boosters inhibit CYP3A4, which raises atorvastatin exposure. Dose limits and monitoring are therefore needed.

The studies are small, and many were terminated or very limited in size. They do not yet establish benefit beyond lipid control, and an antiviral effect is unproven.

## Safety Considerations

- **Drug Interactions:** 100 interactions are recorded. The main ones are:
  - **Major:** clarithromycin.
  - **Moderate:** aprepitant, omeprazole, cimetidine, clotrimazole, dexamethasone, eliglustat, eluxadoline, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, metronidazole, miconazole, naltrexone, pantoprazole, pectin, rosuvastatin and simvastatin.
  - **Minor:** aluminum hydroxide, liraglutide and magnesium hydroxide.
- **HIV-specific interaction risk:** The HIV literature in this report shows that protease inhibitors and boosters raise atorvastatin exposure through CYP3A4 inhibition. This increases the risk of muscle toxicity, so dose limits and monitoring are needed.

Please refer to the package insert for warnings and contraindications, which are not available in the data compiled for this report.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
Several randomized and early-phase studies have tested atorvastatin in people with HIV, but they are small, many were terminated, and the main findings concern lipids and inflammation rather than treating the infection itself. Package insert warnings and contraindications are also missing from the compiled data.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Package insert warnings and contraindications from Medsafe
- Mechanism-of-action data
- Results from larger, adequately powered trials (for example, the 320-patient atorvastatin vs rosuvastatin study) showing clinical benefit
- A dosing and monitoring plan for patients on protease inhibitors or boosted regimens
