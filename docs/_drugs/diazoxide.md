# Diazoxide: From Hyperinsulinaemic Hypoglycaemia to Alopecia

## One-Sentence Summary

Diazoxide is a potassium channel opener, originally used to treat hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia and insulin-producing pancreatic tumours (insulinoma).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Based on known information, diazoxide opens ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channels. In pancreatic beta cells this suppresses insulin release, which explains its use in hyperinsulinaemic hypoglycaemia.

The link to hair disorders comes from a known side effect. Diazoxide causes hypertrichosis (excess hair growth), much like minoxidil, another KATP channel opener that is an established alopecia treatment. In a preclinical study, topical diazoxide stimulated hair regrowth on the bald scalp of stumptailed macaques (PMID 2085505). This is a plausible mechanistic bridge, but it rests on animal data and adverse-effect observations, not human efficacy trials.

The high scores for the other hair-related predictions should be read with caution:
- **Hypotrichosis simplex of the scalp and congenital hypotrichosis milia**: plausibly driven by the same hair-growth mechanism, but no trials or literature exist.
- **Diffuse alopecia areata**: an autoimmune condition, which a KATP opener is unlikely to address.
- **Ambras-type hypertrichosis and hypertrichosis**: these involve excess hair, so promoting hair growth would be counterproductive. The drug causes hypertrichosis rather than treating it.
- **Autosomal dominant hyperinsulinism due to Kir6.2 deficiency**: mechanistically relevant, but it falls within the existing use area (congenital hyperinsulinism), so it is not true repurposing. Dominant KCNJ11 mutations are also often unresponsive to diazoxide.
- **Dandy-Walker malformation syndrome, odontal/periodontal malformation syndrome and genetic hair shaft abnormality**: no plausible mechanism and no evidence, likely knowledge-graph artefacts.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications suggested by the knowledge graph** (not label text): multiple system atrophy, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, orthostatic hypotension, kidney disease and kidney failure, uraemia, pyoureter, liver disease, gallbladder disease, hyperglycaemia, diabetes mellitus, diabetic ketoacidosis, congestive heart failure, potassium deficiency, gout and hyperuricaemia, cerebrovascular disorder, coronary artery disease.
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were identified; the 20 reviewed are all of moderate severity. Main groups:
  - Glucose-lowering drugs: acarbose, metformin, pioglitazone, alogliptin, albiglutide, dulaglutide, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, chlorpropamide, acetohexamide
  - Corticosteroids: hydrocortisone, triamcinolone, dexamethasone, betamethasone, budesonide
  - Others: bupropion, morphine, dronabinol
- **Adverse effects reported in the literature**: hypertrichosis, fluid retention, cardiac failure, necrotising enterocolitis in neonates, and acute renal failure (reported in small series). Systemic exposure is a concern for any hair-related use, so only a topical route would be considered.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
All predictions rest on model scores alone (L5), except alopecia, which has L4 indirect support from animal data and adverse-effect observations. There are no human efficacy trials, the New Zealand licences are all cancelled, and the systemic side-effect burden argues against oral use.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data and the Medsafe package insert warnings and contraindications (required for safety screening)
- Human efficacy data for topical diazoxide in alopecia, or a rationale for choosing it over minoxidil
- Topical formulation feasibility and route compatibility assessment, including systemic absorption and safety
- Rejection of the hypertrichosis and Ambras-type predictions as therapeutic candidates, since diazoxide causes these conditions
